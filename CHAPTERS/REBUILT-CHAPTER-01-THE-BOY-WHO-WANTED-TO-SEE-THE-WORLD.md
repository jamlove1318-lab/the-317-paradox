# CHAPTER 1 — THE BOY WHO WANTED TO SEE THE WORLD

Status: PROVISIONAL — REBUILT OPENING DRAFT

## Page 1

Morning arrived in Veyra the way it always did.

The river complained against the stones.

A baker complained about the river.

A fisherman complained about the baker.

And somewhere between all three complaints, a dog stole a piece of bread.

Gopal was under a wooden cart.

Only his legs were visible.

"Don't move," he said.

Nemi stood over him.

"I'm not moving."

"The cart."

"Oh."

The cart rolled half an inch.

Gopal shouted.

The dog ran away with the bread.

"THAT WAS IMPORTANT!"

Nemi watched the dog disappear.

"It was bread."

"It was MY breakfast."

"You're under a cart."

"Exactly. I had a system."

## Page 2

Gopal emerged with grease across his cheek.

The wheel was still attached.

This was technically an achievement.

Mira stood nearby holding a basket.

She looked at the repaired wheel.

Then at Gopal.

Then at the grease.

"You were told to deliver the basket."

"I was delivering it."

"You've been gone for forty minutes."

"The wheel was broken."

"The basket is still here."

Gopal looked down.

"Oh."

Nemi folded her arms.

"You are the only person I know who can turn carrying bread into mechanical engineering."

"I can do both."

"You did neither."

Mira took the basket from him.

"Breakfast."

Gopal brightened.

"Did you save me some?"

"I saved you exactly what you deserve."

She handed him one piece of bread.

He stared at it.

"This is a very small amount of justice."

## Page 3

Veyra was not a famous town.

It had no palace.

No ancient monument.

No enormous wall.

No legendary battlefield.

It sat where an old inland road met a broad seasonal river, and travelers usually passed through Veyra on their way somewhere else.

Gopal liked that.

He liked that every traveler seemed to know something he didn't.

A woman from the north once said snow made roofs sound different.

A trader from the east claimed there were cities where nobody cooked with salt.

A sailor had insisted that somewhere beyond the western coast, people held arguments while riding giant birds.

Mira had asked why Gopal believed sailors.

"Because they have boats."

"That is not evidence."

"It is better evidence than you have."

Rajan had laughed so hard he nearly dropped a hammer.

Gopal had decided that one day he would see those places himself.

## Page 4

There was one place in Veyra where Gopal could sit for hours.

The notice wall.

It stood beside the old road.

People pinned things to it.

Lost chickens.

Missing tools.

Announcements.

Travel notices.

Arguments written on scraps of paper.

Once, somebody had pinned:

WHOEVER TOOK MY BLUE BUCKET KNOWS EXACTLY WHAT THEY DID.

Underneath it, someone else had written:

I DID NOT.

Under that:

THEN RETURN IT.

Under that:

I DON'T HAVE IT.

Under that:

THEN WHY DID YOU ANSWER?

Gopal had followed the argument for three weeks.

He had no idea who was right.

He considered this one of life's great mysteries.

Nemi found him reading it again.

"Still investigating?"

"Obviously."

"You know you could just ask them."

"Then it stops being mysterious."

Nemi stared at him.

"That is the worst thing you've said today."

"It isn't even noon."

## Page 5

Gopal's collection was kept inside an old wooden box under his bed.

Mira called it rubbish.

Gopal called it important.

There was a river stone.

A broken button.

A bent spoon.

A faded ticket.

A piece of painted wood.

A seed he couldn't identify.

A tiny brass screw.

None of them were valuable.

That was the point.

He remembered where each one came from.

The spoon belonged to a traveler who had eaten at their house during a storm.

The ticket came from a man who had missed his boat.

The painted wood came from a house that had burned down and been rebuilt.

The button came from Nemi's old coat.

Nemi had demanded it back.

Gopal had refused.

"Why?"

"Because if I return it, then I don't have it anymore."

"That is generally how returning things works."

## Page 6

Gopal did not collect things because he believed they were magical.

He collected them because places changed.

People left.

Houses were repaired.

Roads moved.

Children grew up.

Someone could laugh in the same doorway for twenty years and then, one morning, that doorway could belong to somebody else.

Gopal hated that.

He didn't know how to explain it.

So he collected things.

Little pieces of ordinary days.

One afternoon, Rajan found him sorting the box.

"You're going somewhere?"

"No."

"Then why are you packing?"

"I'm not packing."

"You have six objects in a bag."

Gopal looked at the bag.

"That's... organizing."

Rajan smiled.

"Of course."

## Page 7

That afternoon, the river did something strange.

Not dramatically.

No lightning.

No enormous wave.

No voice from the sky.

A floating leaf stopped.

It was caught in the middle of the current.

Gopal noticed because the leaf should have been moving.

He waited.

The water pushed around it.

The leaf stayed still.

Then it moved backward.

Only for a moment.

Upstream.

Gopal stood.

Nemi stood beside him.

Neither spoke.

The leaf drifted backward three body-lengths.

Then it turned.

And continued downstream as if nothing had happened.

Gopal whispered:

"Did you see that?"

Nemi nodded.

"Yes."

"Do you know why?"

"No."

Gopal smiled.

"Good."

Nemi frowned.

"Why is that good?"

"Because now there's something I don't know."

## Page 8

They watched the river for another hour.

Nothing happened.

Gopal threw three sticks into the water.

All three floated normally.

He threw a fourth.

It sank.

He stared.

Nemi said, "That one was a rock."

"I knew that."

"You looked surprised."

"I was testing it."

"You threw a rock."

"Exactly."

Nemi sighed.

A fisherman nearby had watched the whole thing.

"You two planning to discover something?"

Gopal turned.

"Maybe."

The fisherman shrugged.

"Then bring lunch."

That became the official end of the investigation.

Gopal wrote it in his notebook anyway.

**River.**

**Leaf moved upstream.**

**No explanation.**

**Fisherman says bring lunch.**

He underlined the last sentence twice.

## Page 9

The next day, Gopal helped carry sacks from a passing cart.

Mostly because Mira told him to.

One sack split.

Beans rolled everywhere.

Gopal chased them downhill.

A child chased Gopal.

The child chased the beans.

The dog chased everyone.

For ten minutes, the entire eastern market became a disaster.

Gopal eventually stopped the last bean with his foot.

He raised it triumphantly.

"I GOT IT!"

Everyone stared.

Nemi picked up the sack.

"One."

Gopal looked at the bean in his hand.

"One very important bean."

The child took it from him.

"Thanks."

Gopal blinked.

"You're welcome."

He watched the child run away.

Nemi smiled.

"What?"

"You helped."

"Obviously."

"You looked happy."

Gopal shrugged.

"I like fixing things."

Nemi's smile faded slightly.

"Not everything can be fixed."

Gopal didn't answer.

He knew that.

He just didn't like it.

## Page 10

That evening, Gopal climbed onto the roof of his house.

Rajan found him there.

"You know your mother will blame me for letting you climb up."

"You didn't let me."

"That's worse."

They sat together.

Beyond Veyra, the road disappeared over the ridge.

Gopal could see a few distant lights.

"What do you think is there?"

Rajan looked.

"Probably another road."

"Beyond that?"

"Another place."

"Beyond that?"

"Another place."

Gopal smiled.

"How far does it go?"

Rajan thought for a moment.

"Farther than you can walk."

"That's not an answer."

"It is."

Gopal lay back.

"What if I go?"

Rajan didn't laugh.

"Then take enough food."

"That's your advice?"

"Food is underrated."

## Page 11

Gopal expected Rajan to tell him to stay.

Instead, Rajan said:

"If you go, don't go because you think home isn't enough."

Gopal looked at him.

"Home is enough."

"Good."

"I just want to see."

"I know."

"What if I don't come back?"

Rajan was quiet.

"Then I hope you find somewhere worth coming back from."

Gopal sat up.

For once, he didn't have a joke.

Rajan reached into his pocket and handed him a small repair tool.

"You'll need this."

Gopal turned it over.

"Why?"

"Because things break."

"That's your second piece of advice."

"I've spent my life fixing things."

Gopal smiled.

"Okay."

## Page 12

The next morning, Gopal tried to leave.

He made it twenty-seven steps.

Then he came back because he had forgotten his shoes.

Nemi laughed for so long she had to sit down.

"I was testing myself."

"You forgot your shoes."

"Exactly."

"How is that a test?"

"It successfully identified a problem."

"You are not ready."

Gopal put on his shoes.

Then he packed.

Mira removed half the things.

Gopal added them again.

Mira removed them.

Gopal added two.

Mira removed four.

Rajan watched from the doorway.

"How long do you think you'll be gone?"

"Three days."

Mira laughed.

Nemi said, "A week."

Gopal looked offended.

"A week?"

Nemi pointed at his bag.

"You packed enough food for twelve."

Gopal looked down.

"Adventure requires preparation."

## Page 13

That evening, Gopal returned to the notice wall.

The blue bucket argument was gone.

Someone had taken the papers down.

He stared at the empty space.

Nemi stood beside him.

"Sad?"

"A little."

"You never even found out who had the bucket."

"Exactly."

He touched the empty board.

"Now I'll never know."

Nemi looked at him.

"You know, Gopal, you don't have to know everything."

"I know."

"Do you?"

He smiled.

"No."

She laughed.

Then she became serious.

"Are you really leaving?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

Gopal looked toward the road.

"I want to see what is there."

"And then?"

"I don't know."

Nemi nodded.

"That's probably the first honest answer you've given."

## Page 14

The night before he left, Veyra held its little river market.

Nothing special.

That was why Gopal remembered it.

Someone burned the first batch of cakes.

A musician forgot the second verse of a song.

Two old men argued about whether the river had always been wider.

Children chased each other between stalls.

Mira complained that Gopal was eating too much.

Rajan quietly bought him another cake.

Nemi stole half of it.

Gopal accused her of betrayal.

She said:

"You'll survive."

He laughed.

Later, he stood beside the river.

The water moved normally.

The leaf was gone.

He waited.

Nothing happened.

For the first time, Gopal realized he was glad.

If the river had done something impossible again, he might have spent another month trying to understand it.

Instead, it was simply a river.

He liked that too.

## Page 15

At dawn, Gopal left.

Mira hugged him first.

She didn't say anything.

Then she pushed him away.

"Write."

"I will."

"Actually write."

"I will."

"Not just three words."

"I understand."

"You don't."

Rajan hugged him.

"Eat."

"I know."

"Repair things before they become disasters."

"I know."

"Don't argue with people carrying knives."

Gopal considered this.

"That seems strangely specific."

Rajan smiled.

Nemi stood a few steps away.

She held out a blank notebook.

Gopal took it.

"What is this?"

"For things you don't understand."

"I already have a notebook."

"This one is for things you don't understand enough to pretend you understand."

Gopal looked at her.

"That's a very long title."

"Then write smaller."

## Page 16

Gopal walked.

He didn't look back for the first hundred steps.

He thought that would make leaving easier.

It didn't.

At two hundred steps, he looked back.

Veyra was still there.

Smoke from breakfast fires.

The river.

The old road.

The roofs he knew.

The notice wall.

His home.

Nemi was still standing at the edge of town.

She raised one hand.

Gopal raised his.

Then he turned forward.

The road was ordinary.

That disappointed him.

Then it occurred to him that ordinary roads were the ones that led somewhere.

He smiled.

"Okay."

He adjusted his bag.

"Let's see."

## Page 17

For the first hour, adventure was magnificent.

For the second hour, his bag became heavy.

For the third hour, his feet hurt.

For the fourth hour, he discovered that he had forgotten lunch.

Gopal sat beneath a tree.

He opened his bag.

Three spoons.

Two shirts.

A hammer.

Four books.

A cooking pot.

One shoe he did not recognize.

No food.

He stared at the shoe.

"I don't remember packing you."

A bird landed nearby.

Gopal pointed.

"If you know where my lunch is, now would be a good time."

The bird flew away.

"Fine."

He opened the blank notebook.

**Day 1.**

**Important discovery: adventure is hungry.**

He paused.

Then added:

**Also, I miss home.**

## Page 18

A wagon passed.

The driver saw him.

"You lost?"

Gopal stood.

"No."

The driver looked at the bag.

"You're carrying one shoe."

"I know."

"Do you?"

Gopal looked at the shoe.

"Yes."

The driver laughed.

"Where are you going?"

Gopal looked down the road.

"I don't know yet."

"Good answer."

The wagon continued.

Gopal watched it disappear.

Then he noticed something attached to its rear.

A small wooden marker.

Old.

Weathered.

Carved with a simple shape.

Gopal stopped smiling.

He knew that shape.

Not from a story.

Not from the river.

From his collection.

The same shape appeared on the painted piece of wood under his bed.

He reached into his bag.

His hand touched it.

## Page 19

Gopal pulled out the painted piece of wood.

The mark on it was almost identical.

Almost.

He looked at the wagon.

It was already far away.

He could have ignored it.

He almost did.

Then he remembered the river.

The leaf.

The empty notice wall.

The blank notebook.

His father's words.

If you go, don't go because home isn't enough.

Gopal smiled.

He didn't want to chase the wagon because it was mysterious.

He wanted to chase it because it had given him a question.

And questions were why he had left.

He started running.

"WAIT!"

The wagon did not stop.

Gopal ran faster.

"HEY!"

Still nothing.

He laughed.

For the first time since leaving Veyra, he felt completely awake.

## Page 20

The road bent around the hill.

The wagon disappeared.

Gopal stopped.

He was breathing hard.

There were three roads ahead.

One went toward the river.

One toward a forest.

One climbed into the hills.

The wagon was gone.

No sign.

No answer.

No direction.

Gopal looked at the blank notebook.

Then at the three roads.

He smiled.

"Well."

He sat down.

"That's inconvenient."

He opened the notebook.

Under **Day 1**, he wrote:

**First lesson:**

**The world does not owe me directions.**

He thought for a moment.

Then added:

**Second lesson:**

**I still want to see it.**

Far behind him, the river flowed through Veyra.

Normally.

For now.

And somewhere beyond the hill, on a road Gopal had never heard of, someone noticed that the boy carrying the painted piece of wood had finally left home.

But Gopal knew nothing about that.

Not yet.
