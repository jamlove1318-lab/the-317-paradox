# CHAPTER 3 — THE MARKET AT THE END OF THE ROAD

Status: PROVISIONAL — REBUILT OPENING DRAFT

## Page 1

The River Market was supposed to be temporary.

It had been temporary for forty-three years.

Every spring, traders arrived.

Every summer, they complained about the heat.

Every autumn, they argued about prices.

Every winter, they promised not to return.

Every year, they returned.

Gopal arrived just before sunset.

He stopped at the entrance.

There were hundreds of people.

Boats.

Carts.

Lanterns.

Food.

Animals.

Music.

Arguments.

Gopal's eyes widened.

"Wow."

A man beside him said:

"First time?"

"Yes."

"Don't buy anything."

Gopal immediately bought something.

## Page 2

It was a small glass bottle containing blue powder.

The seller said it was ground moonstone.

Gopal asked if that was true.

The seller said absolutely.

A woman passing by said it was dyed salt.

The seller shouted that she was ruining his business.

Gopal bought the salt.

He liked it.

Then he bought fried river cakes.

Then a tiny wooden whistle.

Then a hat.

Then another hat.

Then he realized he had almost no money left.

He looked at his purchases.

"This is a problem."

A boy carrying baskets said:

"You have a bigger problem."

"What?"

"You're wearing the hat."

Gopal looked down.

The hat was enormous.

It covered one eye.

He liked it more.

## Page 3

Gopal spent the next hour wandering.

He found spices from three regions.

A woman selling carved shells.

A man repairing clocks.

Children racing toy boats through gutters.

A family arguing over whether their goat had eaten a merchant's scarf.

The goat looked innocent.

Gopal did not trust it.

He bought noodles.

Then someone bumped him.

His notebook fell.

He picked it up.

A page was missing.

Gopal froze.

He searched the ground.

Nothing.

He searched beneath the stall.

Nothing.

He checked his bag.

Nothing.

His stomach tightened.

The page contained his drawing of the painted mark.

## Page 4

Gopal searched the market.

He asked the spice seller.

No.

The clockmaker.

No.

The goat owner.

The goat looked guilty.

"No."

He followed a child carrying paper.

Not his.

He followed a musician.

Not his.

He climbed onto a barrel.

The market became a sea of heads.

Then he saw it.

A piece of paper stuck to a lantern.

His drawing.

Gopal jumped down.

A hand grabbed it first.

An older girl stood holding the page.

She looked at the symbol.

Then at Gopal.

"Why are you drawing this?"

Gopal reached for the page.

"Because I found it."

"Where?"

He hesitated.

She folded it.

"Interesting."

"Can I have it back?"

She smiled.

"Maybe."

## Page 5

Her name was **Ilen**.

She was nineteen.

She worked with a traveling group that bought and sold handmade instruments.

She knew dozens of roads.

She had been to places Gopal had never heard of.

She also knew the symbol.

Not what it meant.

Where it appeared.

"Old bridges."

Gopal remembered Savi.

"How many?"

"Seven that I know."

"Where?"

"All over."

"Why?"

Ilen shrugged.

"People carve things on bridges."

"Do they carve this?"

"Sometimes."

"Why?"

She smiled.

"Now you're asking the right question."

## Page 6

Ilen handed the page back.

"I've seen it on bridges that were repaired recently."

"Recently?"

"Some were hundreds of years old."

Gopal frowned.

"Then why would the same mark appear on them?"

"Maybe it doesn't mean anything."

Gopal stared at her.

She laughed.

"You're disappointed."

"A little."

"Good."

"Why?"

"Because the world is more interesting when you don't get an answer every time you ask a question."

Gopal thought about that.

He didn't like it.

He suspected she was right.

## Page 7

A bell rang.

The entire market changed.

Stalls closed.

Boats pulled away.

People began moving toward the river.

Gopal followed.

"What's happening?"

Ilen answered:

"The Lantern Run."

"What's that?"

"You'll see."

Hundreds of small floating lanterns were placed on the water.

Each one carried a name.

Some were names of people.

Some were names of places.

Some were simply words.

A child placed one on the river.

"What's yours?"

Gopal asked.

The child answered:

"Tomorrow."

## Page 8

Gopal stared.

"That's a strange name."

"It's not a name."

The child shrugged.

"It's what I want."

Gopal smiled.

He liked that answer.

Ilen watched the lanterns.

"Every year people write something they want to carry forward."

"Does it work?"

"No."

"Then why do it?"

"Because people aren't machines."

Gopal looked at the river.

Hundreds of lights moved together.

For a moment, he forgot about the symbol.

## Page 9

A lantern struck a rock.

It went out.

The person who launched it sighed.

Gopal moved toward the river.

Ilen stopped him.

"Don't."

"I can fix it."

"You can't fix every lantern."

Gopal looked at her.

Then he stopped.

The lantern drifted away.

He hated watching it.

But he didn't jump into the river.

That mattered.

Ilen noticed.

She didn't say anything.

## Page 10

Later, Gopal sat beside the market fire.

He ate noodles.

The enormous hat sat beside him.

Ilen looked at it.

"You're keeping that?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"I paid for it."

"That's not a reason."

"It is now."

They laughed.

Gopal told her about Veyra.

Not everything.

Just the market.

The notice wall.

His parents.

Nemi.

The river.

Ilen listened.

Then she told him about the instrument caravan.

Her dream was to travel until she could identify the origin of every traditional instrument she heard.

Not own them.

Not sell them.

Understand where the sounds came from.

Gopal liked that.

"That's a very specific dream."

"So is yours."

"What is mine?"

"You haven't figured it out yet."

## Page 11

That night, Gopal slept beside the market fire.

He dreamed about roads.

Not magical roads.

Normal roads.

Thousands of them.

People walking in different directions.

Nobody knowing where all the roads went.

He woke before sunrise.

The painted piece of wood was beside his hand.

He frowned.

He hadn't taken it out.

He put it back in his bag.

## Page 12

Morning brought rain.

The market became chaos.

Water poured through the streets.

Merchants shouted.

Animals escaped.

A boat floated through a vegetable stall.

Gopal watched it pass.

"That seems bad."

Ilen shouted from across the street:

"IT IS!"

Gopal ran.

The river had risen overnight.

A temporary footbridge had shifted.

People were stranded on the opposite side.

Gopal looked at it.

Then at the bridge.

He remembered Savi.

## Page 13

The bridge was badly designed.

Gopal could tell that now.

Not because he knew bridges.

Because Savi had taught him to look.

The supports were pulling against each other.

One rope had already snapped.

Gopal shouted:

"Nobody cross!"

A merchant shouted back:

"Then how do we get the carts?"

Gopal looked around.

He saw barrels.

Ropes.

A broken cart.

Wooden poles.

He remembered the handcart in Bellwater.

He started organizing people.

"Move those barrels!"

"Why?"

"Because I said so!"

The merchant glared.

"That's not convincing."

Gopal paused.

"Because if we don't, the bridge falls."

That worked.

## Page 14

For twenty minutes, everyone worked.

Not perfectly.

Not gracefully.

A barrel rolled into the river.

Someone fell into the mud.

A goat ate a rope.

Gopal shouted at it.

The goat ignored him.

They built a temporary support.

The bridge stopped moving.

The stranded people crossed one at a time.

When the last child reached the market side, everyone cheered.

Gopal smiled.

Then the bridge collapsed.

## Page 15

Silence.

Nobody was hurt.

But three carts were destroyed.

One belonged to a woman who had been saving for years to replace it.

She looked at Gopal.

"You told us it was safe."

Gopal's smile disappeared.

"I thought it would hold."

"You thought."

She stared at the broken cart.

"That's not the same as knowing."

Gopal had no answer.

Ilen stood beside him.

She didn't defend him.

That hurt.

But she stayed.

## Page 16

Gopal helped repair what could be repaired.

He sold his new hat.

Then the wooden whistle.

Then the moonstone.

Which turned out to be dyed salt.

The seller refused to buy it back.

By evening, Gopal had almost no money.

The woman with the broken cart took the money.

She didn't forgive him.

She didn't thank him.

She simply said:

"Next time, ask someone who knows."

Gopal nodded.

"I will."

## Page 17

Ilen found him later.

"You did something good."

Gopal looked at her.

"Did I?"

"You stopped the bridge long enough to get people across."

"Then broke it."

"You didn't break it."

"I helped."

"You also guessed."

Gopal looked at the river.

"I thought wanting to help was enough."

Ilen sat beside him.

"It's a good beginning."

"Not a good ending."

"No."

She smiled.

"That's why people learn."

## Page 18

The next morning, the market began packing.

Ilen's caravan was leaving west.

Gopal asked:

"Where are you going?"

"Three towns."

"Then?"

"Another three."

"Can I come?"

Ilen shook her head.

"Not this time."

Gopal looked disappointed.

She continued:

"You don't know what you want yet."

"I want to see the world."

"So do I."

She pointed at his notebook.

"You should find out what you want to do while seeing it."

Gopal nodded.

That answer stayed with him.

## Page 19

Before leaving, Ilen handed him something.

A tiny wooden tuning peg.

"What's this?"

"From an instrument made in the northern valleys."

"Why give it to me?"

"Because you collect rubbish."

Gopal smiled.

"Important things."

"Right."

She climbed into the caravan.

Then called:

"Gopal!"

He looked up.

"Don't let the world turn into a list."

"What does that mean?"

Ilen smiled.

"You'll figure it out."

## Page 20

The caravan disappeared west.

Gopal stayed by the river.

He opened his notebook.

**Day 4.**

**Savi taught me that bridges need to hold.**

**Ilen taught me that questions don't always need answers.**

He thought about the woman with the broken cart.

Then wrote:

**I learned that wanting to help is not the same as knowing how.**

He closed the notebook.

The painted piece of wood remained warm inside his bag.

Gopal left it there.

The road ahead was ordinary.

For now.

And somewhere far away, an old bridge was being repaired.

On one of its stones, almost hidden beneath fresh mortar, the same small mark waited.

Not for Gopal.

Not yet.
