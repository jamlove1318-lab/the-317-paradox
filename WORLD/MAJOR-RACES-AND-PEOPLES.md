# MAJOR RACES & PEOPLES — LIVING WORLD FOUNDATION

> Project: Gopal original adventure manga
> Status: PROVISIONAL WORLD DESIGN
> Principle: Species are biological peoples; cultures are historical solutions, not costumes.
> Active story direction: Adventure-first. The world must remain interesting even when the legendary destination is not involved.

---

## 0. DESIGN LAW

The world will not use the simple fantasy formula:

> race = appearance + one personality + one homeland + one job.

Instead:

> biology → environmental possibilities/constraints → migration → history → culture → institutions → internal disagreement.

A species can contain dozens of cultures.

A culture can contain multiple species.

A person can reject their own culture.

A city can be more mixed than a continent.

A race can be famous for something without every individual being good at it.

No species exists only to:
- be comic relief;
- be a villain;
- be a warrior;
- be a wise ancient people;
- be a servant race;
- be a mysterious race;
- be a love interest;
- provide a crew role.

The reader should eventually meet ordinary farmers, children, teachers, criminals, musicians, politicians, doctors, athletes, merchants, soldiers, cooks, artists, cowards, heroes, idiots, saints, bullies and kind people from every major people.

---

# 1. HUMANS — THE WIDESPREAD PEOPLE

## Biological identity

Humans are the most geographically widespread sapient people in the known world.

They are not the default "normal" species. They are simply the people whose history produced the widest migration networks.

Humans vary enormously between regions because they have repeatedly migrated, mixed, traded and settled.

## Why they spread

Humans are unusually adaptable in social organization.

They build tools, learn foreign techniques, form mixed communities, travel long distances and rapidly change occupations when environments change.

This does NOT mean humans are inherently superior.

Their greatest strength is cultural flexibility.

Their greatest weakness is that they can turn flexibility into exploitation.

## Culture

There is no single human culture.

A Westerly fishing town and a Northern imperial capital may have almost nothing culturally in common.

A human merchant may speak three languages and eat food invented by another species.

A human family may preserve a custom older than their kingdom.

## Common social patterns

Humans frequently form:
- guilds;
- city-states;
- kingdoms;
- republics;
- trading leagues;
- villages;
- nomadic communities;
- mixed-species ports.

## Reputation

Other peoples often see humans as:
- ambitious;
- adaptable;
- unpredictable;
- numerous;
- good traders;
- dangerous when organized.

Some cultures admire them.

Some distrust them.

Some barely care.

## Story use

Humans give us the widest variety of ordinary civilization.

They also prevent the story from turning every cultural difference into species biology.

# 2. THE VARKAI — PEOPLE OF THE RESONANT SENSE

## Core biological idea

Varkai possess highly sensitive structures along the jaw, throat and upper chest that detect subtle vibration.

They can hear normally, but they also perceive vibrations through surfaces and air in ways humans cannot.

Their ability is biological, not supernatural.

A Varkai can sometimes notice:
- footsteps through a floor;
- machinery vibrating irregularly;
- distant construction;
- changes in a ship's hull;
- approaching animals through ground vibration.

They cannot magically "sense everything."

Noise, crowds, storms and deliberately confusing vibration can overwhelm them.

## Origin environments

Their earliest populations developed around:
- canyon systems;
- stone valleys;
- cliff settlements;
- large underground chambers;
- regions where vibration carried farther than sight.

## Architecture

Varkai architecture often emphasizes:
- resonant stone;
- suspended walkways;
- hollow walls;
- vibration-safe foundations;
- public floors designed to carry civic signals.

Some old cities have ceremonial "listening halls" where announcements are transmitted through architecture.

## Language

Varkai languages often use rhythm and duration as important grammatical features.

A sentence spoken quickly and slowly can carry different social implications.

Their writing traditionally developed as patterned marks rather than simple alphabetic notation.

## Culture

One old Varkai custom is the "First Knock":

When entering someone's home, a visitor taps a particular rhythm rather than simply calling out.

Families develop their own versions.

A child learning the family's knock is treated as a rite of belonging.

## Internal conflict

Some Varkai communities consider their heightened perception a responsibility.

Others reject that idea:

> "I can hear you. That does not mean I owe you an answer."

This creates interesting tensions around privacy, surveillance and trust.

## Occupations

Common Varkai professions include:
- architects;
- miners;
- musicians;
- ship inspectors;
- builders;
- engineers;
- judges;
- messengers.

But many are simply farmers, shopkeepers, children, teachers and ordinary workers.

## Story possibilities

A Varkai character might be:
- a terrible fighter;
- a brilliant drummer;
- a nervous doctor;
- a dishonest merchant;
- a hilarious loudmouth;
- a quiet child who hates being able to hear adults arguing through walls.

Their biology should create story possibilities, not personality destiny.

---

# 3. THE TALASSI — PEOPLE OF WATER AND LAND

## Core biological idea

Talassi are semi-aquatic sapient beings.

They can breathe air and extract oxygen from water through specialized structures around the neck and rib area.

Their bodies tolerate pressure changes better than humans, but they are not invulnerable deep-sea creatures.

They need regular access to water.

Their skin changes texture depending on hydration and temperature.

## Homeland

Talassi populations developed around:
- deltas;
- mangrove coasts;
- river cities;
- archipelagos;
- shallow seas;
- floodplains.

## Civilization

Talassi civilization is not "underwater kingdom."

Most major Talassi cities are amphibious.

Homes may have:
- dry upper rooms;
- submerged lower rooms;
- water courtyards;
- floating markets;
- canal streets.

Children often learn swimming before walking confidently.

## Food

Talassi cuisine is famous for:
- fermented shell foods;
- river vegetables;
- salt breads;
- smoked fish;
- floating herb gardens;
- seasonal freshwater dishes.

Different regions disagree fiercely about what counts as proper cooking.

## Social structure

Many traditional Talassi communities organize time around water cycles.

But modern Talassi cities may use clocks, trade schedules and standardized calendars.

This creates generational tension.

## Internal conflict

Some Talassi activists argue that major land governments treat waterways as property.

Others are wealthy merchants who profit from the same systems.

The species therefore has class conflict, political disagreement and regional identities.

## Occupations

- sailors;
- divers;
- engineers;
- farmers;
- fishers;
- doctors;
- port officials;
- traders;
- musicians;
- rescue workers.

## Story possibilities

A Talassi city can create:
- canal chases;
- underwater rescue sequences;
- flood disasters;
- festivals;
- ordinary romance;
- trade disputes;
- sports;
- naval politics.

It does not need a mystery.

---

# 4. THE KORRUN — THE DENSE-BONED PEOPLE

## Core biological idea

Korrun have unusually dense skeletal structures and powerful connective tissue.

They are generally heavier than humans of similar size.

They tolerate impacts and difficult terrain well, but their bodies require more food and their joints can suffer from long-term strain.

They are not "strong race = best fighters."

A Korrun accountant may hate violence.

A Korrun child may be physically fragile.

A Korrun athlete may be spectacular.

## Homeland

Historically concentrated in:
- high plateaus;
- cold valleys;
- volcanic regions;
- rugged interior settlements.

## Architecture

Korrun buildings often emphasize:
- low centers of gravity;
- broad stairs;
- heavy foundations;
- heat-retaining walls;
- communal halls.

Their old engineering traditions are famous.

But their architecture is not all stone castles.

Modern Korrun cities can contain glass towers, wooden houses and crowded markets.

## Culture

A major Korrun tradition is communal repair.

When something breaks in an old village, repairing it is often treated as a social event.

Children learn basic maintenance from older relatives.

This created a cultural respect for:
- craft;
- maintenance;
- patience;
- durable objects.

But younger Korrun increasingly embrace disposable consumer culture.

Older generations complain.

Young people roll their eyes.

Exactly as they should.

## Food

High-energy food is common:
- dense breads;
- root vegetables;
- smoked meats;
- mountain cheeses;
- hot stews;
- mineral-rich soups.

## Internal conflict

Traditional craft guilds sometimes clash with industrial corporations.

Some Korrun see their heritage as something to protect.

Others see it as a cage.

## Occupations

- builders;
- mechanics;
- farmers;
- miners;
- athletes;
- cooks;
- machinists;
- teachers;
- soldiers.

## Story possibilities

A Korrun settlement can host:
- mountain races;
- engineering competitions;
- labor disputes;
- family drama;
- festivals;
- mining disasters;
- giant machines;
- ordinary school life.

---

# 5. THE SAARU — THE NIGHT-EYED PEOPLE

## Core biological idea

Saaru evolved in regions where low-light survival was important.

They have highly sensitive vision and a strong ability to distinguish movement in darkness.

Bright light can overwhelm them.

Their eyes adapt slowly between light levels.

They sleep differently depending on region and occupation.

## Homeland

Ancient Saaru populations developed in:
- desert-edge regions;
- dense night forests;
- long-winter territories;
- cave-border settlements.

Modern Saaru live worldwide.

## Culture

Because night travel historically mattered, many Saaru cultures developed vibrant night economies.

Markets open after sunset.

Festivals use lantern choreography.

Nighttime storytelling is important.

Children may have "dark games" where they navigate familiar streets by sound and memory.

## Architecture

Common features:
- shaded courtyards;
- indirect lighting;
- colored lanterns;
- reflective surfaces;
- dark interior spaces.

A Saaru city at midnight can be one of the most beautiful places in the world.

## Internal conflict

Some Saaru communities are proud of their night traditions.

Others want ordinary daytime schedules because international commerce and schools often favor human-majority systems.

This is a recurring source of political pressure.

## Occupations

No fixed stereotype.

Saaru can be:
- teachers;
- artists;
- farmers;
- astronomers;
- sailors;
- guards;
- performers;
- merchants;
- children.

## Story possibilities

Their night culture can create entire adventure arcs without supernatural mystery.

---

# 6. THE EYRIN — THE WIND-BORN PEOPLE

## Core biological idea

Eyrin have lightweight skeletal structures, powerful balance systems and a flexible membrane structure extending from the arms and torso.

They cannot naturally fly like birds.

They can glide and control falls extremely well.

Some cultures developed glider technology that turns this biological advantage into true aerial travel.

## Homeland

They historically lived around:
- steep mountain regions;
- cliff settlements;
- enormous canyon networks;
- high forests.

## Architecture

Eyrin settlements often grow vertically.

Homes may connect through:
- bridges;
- ropeways;
- terraces;
- landing platforms;
- wind towers.

Ground-level architecture is often less important than vertical routes.

## Culture

Eyrin communities historically treated falling as a practical skill rather than a metaphor.

Children learn:
- balance;
- climbing;
- rope work;
- emergency landing;
- wind reading.

Their festivals can involve enormous synchronized glider displays.

## Internal conflict

Some Eyrin traditionalists believe technology is making their people careless.

Others argue:

> "If humans can build ships, why can't we build better wings?"

The debate produces inventors, athletes and reckless teenagers.

## Occupations

- couriers;
- climbers;
- architects;
- rescue workers;
- pilots;
- performers;
- farmers;
- engineers.

## Story possibilities

Eyrin regions give us:
- vertical cities;
- aerial races;
- mountain rescue;
- sky festivals;
- cliff battles;
- ridiculous comedy involving Gopal trying to glide.

---

# 7. THE MORU — THE LONG-LIVED PEOPLE

## Core biological idea

Moru mature more slowly than humans and generally live much longer.

Their memory is not automatically perfect.

This distinction is important.

A 150-year-old Moru can still forget things, misremember events, become biased or be wrong.

## Homeland

Moru populations historically concentrated in:
- old forests;
- temperate valleys;
- river civilizations;
- long-lived agricultural regions.

They later spread through trade and scholarship.

## Culture

Because generations overlap for longer periods, Moru families can contain:
- great-grandparents;
- grandparents;
- parents;
- children;
- grandchildren

all living at once.

This creates complicated family politics.

A Moru may know stories from six generations personally.

But this also creates pressure.

A young Moru can be told:

> "Your great-grandmother made that mistake too."

And answer:

> "I don't care."

## Architecture

Moru buildings often accumulate layers.

A house can contain additions built by different generations.

Old buildings are not automatically sacred.

Some are abandoned.

Some are renovated.

Some are demolished.

## Politics

Long lifespans create unusual political arguments.

A 70-year-old human politician may be seen as old.

A 70-year-old Moru politician might be considered young.

This can create disputes over representation and power.

## Internal conflict

Moru society has a major generational divide between:
- preservationists;
- reformers.

Neither side is universally right.

## Occupations

Everything.

Especially:
- historians;
- farmers;
- teachers;
- artists;
- politicians;
- craftsmen;
- doctors.

But many Moru simply run shops.

## Story possibilities

A Moru character can know a historical figure personally without being an exposition machine.

They may remember the event incorrectly.

They may have hated the famous person.

They may refuse to talk about it.

---

# 8. THE NEM — THE MANY-FORM PEOPLE

## Core biological idea

Nem bodies have unusually flexible tissue and limited voluntary control over pigmentation and surface texture.

They cannot shapeshift into arbitrary bodies.

They can alter:
- skin coloration;
- surface pattern;
- minor facial contour;
- texture.

This evolved partly for communication and environmental adaptation.

## Culture

Nem societies often developed sophisticated visual languages.

Clothing is important.

Body patterns can communicate:
- celebration;
- mourning;
- profession;
- family;
- courtship;
- political affiliation.

But modern Nem people may ignore these traditions completely.

## Architecture

Nem cities use:
- patterned walls;
- changing light;
- textured surfaces;
- public art;
- color-coded districts.

## Social issue

Their appearance makes them vulnerable to stereotyping and suspicion.

Some governments use biometric laws aggressively against them.

Some Nem communities respond by becoming extremely privacy-conscious.

Others embrace public identity.

## Occupations

Again, everything.

But some are famous:
- artists;
- actors;
- designers;
- diplomats;
- spies;
- teachers;
- criminals.

The last category is not because they are naturally deceptive.

It is because governments and fiction historically made that stereotype profitable.

The story should actively show Nem people who are terrible at lying.

## Story possibilities

Nem culture gives us:
- masquerade festivals;
- identity comedy;
- political conflict;
- fashion;
- visual art;
- mistaken identity;
- characters confronting prejudice.

# 9. THE IMPORTANT RULE: SPECIES ARE NOT COUNTRIES

A Varkai city may contain:
- Varkai;
- humans;
- Talassi;
- Korrun;
- Saaru;
- Eyrin;
- Moru;
- Nem.

A Talassi kingdom may have a human minority.

A Korrun mining town may employ Saaru engineers.

An Eyrin capital may be mostly mixed-species.

A Moru family may have lived among humans for four generations.

This creates a world where species and nationality are separate concepts.

---

# 10. SPECIES + CULTURE + CLASS

Every major people should eventually contain:

### At least three cultural traditions
Different regions should disagree.

### At least two major historical migrations
People should not remain frozen in ancestral homelands.

### At least one internal political conflict
No species is politically unified.

### At least one stereotype other peoples hold about them
Some stereotypes will be partially true, some outdated, some completely wrong.

### At least one stereotype they hold about themselves
And at least one younger generation that challenges it.

### At least one major mixed city
Where species identity is less important than occupation, neighborhood, class, language or family.

### At least one historical tragedy
Not necessarily species-wide oppression.

It could be:
- civil war;
- famine;
- migration;
- plague;
- industrial accident;
- religious conflict;
- colonial expansion;
- ecological collapse;
- political betrayal.

### At least one beautiful tradition
Something readers genuinely want to experience.

---

# 11. SPECIES RELATIONSHIPS

The world should NOT have a simple:

> humans vs everyone else

history.

Instead:

- two species may have been allies in one century and enemies in another;
- two cultures of the same species may hate each other;
- three species may share one religion;
- a trade language may be spoken across five species;
- mixed families may be normal in some regions and controversial in others;
- old wars may still affect modern borders;
- some people may not care about species at all;
- some governments may discriminate;
- some individuals may exploit species differences politically.

History creates relationships.

Biology does not decide morality.

---

# 12. LANGUAGE

We will not create eight completely separate constructed languages immediately.

Instead, the world will develop:

- major trade languages;
- regional dialects;
- old ceremonial languages;
- species-specific languages;
- mixed port slang;
- professional jargon;
- writing systems;
- extinct languages.

A child in a major port may naturally speak three languages.

A remote village may speak a dialect that outsiders struggle to understand.

Language will sometimes create:
- comedy;
- misunderstandings;
- intimacy;
- exclusion;
- political conflict;
- clues about migration.

---

# 13. FOOD AS WORLD-BUILDING

Food will be one of our strongest tools.

Every major region should eventually answer:

- What is the staple grain/carbohydrate?
- What grows locally?
- What animals are eaten?
- What is considered luxury food?
- What food is associated with mourning?
- What food is eaten at festivals?
- What food do children love?
- What food do adults pretend to like?
- What food is hated by another culture?
- What dishes spread through trade?
- What dish was invented during a historical crisis?

Gopal should eventually have favorite foods from multiple cultures.

Some companions should argue about food.

Some should cook.

Some should be terrible cooks.

Food should make the world feel bigger.

---

# 14. RELIGION AND BELIEF

We will not make one universal mythology explaining everything.

There should be:
- local gods;
- ancestor traditions;
- philosophical schools;
- nature beliefs;
- organized religions;
- secular societies;
- superstition;
- contradictory creation stories;
- people who believe none of it.

Some religions can share stories but interpret them differently.

Some legends can be based on real historical events.

Some can be completely fictional.

The story should never require every belief system to secretly be about the legendary destination.

---

# 15. SPECIES AND THE POWER SYSTEM

Species biology is NOT the supernatural power system.

A Talassi does not control water because they are Talassi.

A Varkai does not have supernatural vibration powers because of biology.

An Eyrin does not automatically have wind magic.

Some individuals may later possess supernatural abilities, but those abilities should be explainable through the separate power system.

This separation matters.

Otherwise the world becomes:

> race = magic type.

We do not want that.

# 16. WHAT THIS GIVES OUR ADVENTURE MANGA

Now a single journey can move through:

- a Talassi river republic;
- a Varkai canyon city;
- a Korrun mountain festival;
- a human trade empire;
- a Saaru night market;
- an Eyrin cliff civilization;
- a Moru agricultural valley;
- a Nem artistic capital;

without every location being a mystery island.

Each place can have its own:
- food;
- sports;
- jokes;
- political problems;
- occupations;
- architecture;
- families;
- local villains;
- heroes;
- festivals;
- history;
- music;
- weather;
- wildlife;
- ordinary people.

That is the scale of world we want.

---

# 17. FUTURE SPECIES — DO NOT OVERFILL THE WORLD YET

These eight are the initial major sapient peoples.

We deliberately leave room for:

- rare isolated peoples;
- extinct peoples;
- disputed species;
- ancient species;
- non-humanoid intelligent beings;
- peoples discovered later;
- species with very small populations;
- beings whose sapience is debated;
- artificial/constructed beings if the technology later supports them.

Do NOT create all of them now.

The world should have empty space.

Some races should be rare enough that meeting one is an event.

Others should be so common that nobody comments on them.

# 18. CREATIVE INFLUENCE RULE

Our inspiration pool is intentionally broad:

- evolutionary biology;
- anthropology;
- archaeology;
- maritime cultures;
- mountain cultures;
- desert societies;
- migration history;
- folklore;
- comparative mythology;
- animal adaptations;
- architecture;
- food history;
- languages;
- games;
- comics;
- manga/anime;
- science fiction;
- fantasy;
- adventure cinema.

The goal is not to reproduce any source.

We take underlying principles:

> "What if biology changed communication?"

> "What if geography changed architecture?"

> "What if a long lifespan changed politics?"

> "What if night became the center of urban life?"

> "What if migration created mixed cultures?"

> "What if a physical adaptation became a social tradition?"

Then we build our own answer.

# 19. FINAL QUALITY TEST

Before a race becomes important in the manga, we ask:

1. Can I imagine a child from this people?
2. Can I imagine an annoying teenager from this people?
3. Can I imagine an elderly person?
4. Can I imagine a coward?
5. Can I imagine a criminal?
6. Can I imagine a genuinely kind person?
7. Can I imagine someone who hates their own culture?
8. Can I imagine someone fiercely proud of it?
9. Can I imagine two people of this species who completely disagree?
10. Can I imagine what they eat?
11. Can I imagine their homes?
12. Can I imagine their weddings?
13. Can I imagine their funerals?
14. Can I imagine their jokes?
15. Can I imagine their children playing?
16. Can I imagine their politics?
17. Can I imagine what they fear?
18. Can I imagine what they love?
19. Would they still exist if Gopal never visited?
20. Could a reader remember them without reducing them to one stereotype?

If not, the species is not finished.

# 20. STATUS

All species names, biology and cultural details above are:

**PROVISIONAL.**

The important CANON direction is the design philosophy:

> **The world contains multiple intelligent peoples whose biology, geography, history, migration and culture interact—but no species is morally, politically or narratively monolithic.**

Next dependency:

**MAJOR CIVILIZATIONS & CULTURAL REGIONS**

We should now design civilizations rather than simply adding more species.

The next layer will answer:

- Which kingdoms/republics/empires/free territories exist?
- Which species dominate which regions?
- Which regions are mixed?
- Which civilizations survived the Last Expedition?
- Which countries cooperate with the World Patrol?
- Which reject it?
- Where are the great trade routes?
- Where are the dangerous frontiers?
- Where do ordinary people live?
- Which civilizations are currently rising?
- Which are declining?
- Which wars are recent?
- Which festivals are famous worldwide?
- Which cities are legendary for reasons completely unrelated to the final destination?

That is where the world will start feeling like a real planet rather than a list of races.
