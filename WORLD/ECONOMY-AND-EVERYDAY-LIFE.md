# WORLD — ECONOMY & EVERYDAY LIFE

> Status: PROVISIONAL
> Purpose: make the world function as a lived-in civilization rather than a sequence of adventure locations.
> Core principle: ordinary life must remain interesting even when no legendary destination, supernatural power or major villain is involved.

---

# 1. THE ECONOMIC DESIGN LAW

The world economy should answer one simple question:

> **How does an ordinary person live?**

Not every person is an adventurer.

Most people:
- work;
- eat;
- sleep;
- raise children;
- pay for things;
- repair things;
- fall in love;
- argue;
- celebrate;
- get sick;
- worry about money;
- hope their children have better lives.

Gopal's adventure becomes more meaningful when the reader understands what he is disrupting, protecting or simply passing through.

---

# 2. MONEY

## Working currency system

The world uses a widely recognized international currency called the **Crown** — PROVISIONAL.

Local governments also issue regional coins and notes.

The Crown is useful for:
- international trade;
- port fees;
- ship purchases;
- large contracts;
- bounty payments;
- banking;
- long-distance travel.

Local currencies remain important.

This prevents the world from feeling economically identical everywhere.

## Important rule

Do not make prices mathematically identical across the world.

A Crown should buy different amounts depending on:
- region;
- scarcity;
- season;
- war;
- distance;
- local production;
- transport cost.

Food can be cheap in a farming valley and expensive at a remote port.

---

# 3. BANKING

Banks exist in major civilizations.

Services include:
- savings;
- loans;
- currency exchange;
- trade financing;
- letters of credit;
- insurance;
- inheritance management.

Remote regions rely more on:
- merchant houses;
- trusted brokers;
- community credit;
- physical valuables.

A traveler carrying all their money physically is risky.

This creates natural adventure problems.

# 5. SHIPS ARE AN ECONOMIC SYSTEM

Ships are not disposable adventure props.

A ship requires:
- wood or other structural material;
- metal;
- rope;
- sails;
- engines where applicable;
- navigation instruments;
- food storage;
- water;
- maintenance;
- trained crew.

A serious ship can represent years of savings.

Losing one can devastate a family, business or organization.

## Ship industries

Major ports contain:
- shipyards;
- repair docks;
- sailmakers;
- ropewalks;
- metalworkers;
- carpenters;
- navigators;
- marine engineers;
- chandlers;
- food suppliers.

This gives maritime cities their own economies.

---

# 6. INSURANCE

Insurance is important because the world is dangerous.

Policies can cover:
- cargo;
- ships;
- warehouses;
- workers;
- trade routes;
- expedition equipment.

Insurance companies hate:
- pirates;
- supernatural disasters;
- irresponsible captains;
- Gopal.

A captain who repeatedly destroys ships may eventually discover that nobody wants to insure him.

This can become comedy as well as a genuine economic consequence.

---

# 7. ADVENTURE IS AN INDUSTRY

The legendary destination created an enormous exploration economy.

Businesses sell:
- maps;
- equipment;
- expedition food;
- navigation tools;
- climbing gear;
- survival equipment;
- translation services;
- guides;
- ships;
- training;
- historical books;
- souvenirs.

Some companies genuinely support exploration.

Others sell worthless products to hopeful adventurers.

Examples:
- fake "authentic" expedition maps;
- miracle navigation compasses;
- fake Passage Key replicas;
- destination-themed restaurants;
- children's toys;
- fortune-telling services;
- fraudulent expedition sponsorships.

The world can be fascinated by the legendary destination without every person actually caring about reaching it.

# 9. FOOD

Food is one of the strongest ways to make civilizations feel different.

Every region should have:
- staple foods;
- street food;
- cheap meals;
- festival dishes;
- preserved foods;
- desserts;
- drinks;
- foods associated with class;
- foods associated with religion;
- foods created through migration.

Trade means ingredients travel.

But local geography still matters.

A mountain city should not eat exactly like a tropical port.

## Gopal's relationship with food

Gopal should genuinely care about eating.

Food becomes:
- comedy;
- friendship;
- cultural discovery;
- crew bonding;
- conflict;
- memory.

A meal can become more memorable than a treasure.

# 11. INFORMATION AND NEWS

Information travels unevenly.

Methods include:
- newspapers;
- messengers;
- postal ships;
- public notice boards;
- traveling performers;
- merchants;
- radio-like technology in advanced regions — PROVISIONAL;
- letters;
- rumor.

A major event can take weeks or months to reach a remote community.

This creates different versions of the same event.

Gopal might become famous in one city while a village thousands of miles away has never heard of him.

---

# 12. NEWSPAPERS

Newspapers are important political and cultural institutions.

Different papers have different:
- owners;
- political positions;
- regional interests;
- standards;
- audiences.

Some are respected.

Some sensationalize.

Some print rumors.

Some investigate corruption.

Some mainly publish:
- sports;
- fashion;
- business;
- entertainment.

This prevents "the newspaper" from being one universal narrator.

---

# 13. COMMUNICATION TECHNOLOGY

The world is technologically uneven.

Possible systems include:
- letters;
- telegraph-like networks;
- long-distance signal towers;
- wired communication in major cities;
- specialized maritime communication;
- rare advanced devices.

Technology should not erase geography.

A message should still sometimes arrive late.

Remote regions should remain remote.

---

# 14. TECHNOLOGY LEVEL

The setting should feel like a world undergoing technological transition.

It contains:
- traditional crafts;
- sailing ships;
- steam-like machinery;
- industrial factories;
- advanced mechanical devices;
- specialized scientific equipment;
- rare supernatural/technological hybrids.

Technology is unevenly distributed.

A wealthy city might have advanced public infrastructure while a remote village still relies on hand tools.

This allows different visual cultures without making the world feel technologically random.

---

# 15. INDUSTRIALIZATION

Industrial expansion is changing the world.

Effects include:
- factories;

- mining expansion;
- mass production;
- faster shipping;
- urbanization;
- pollution;
- labor movements;
- new middle classes;
- wealth concentration;
- cheaper manufactured goods.

Not every civilization embraces this equally.

Some regions welcome industry.

Some regulate it heavily.

Some resist it.

Some depend on it despite disliking it.

# 17. SOCIAL CLASS

Class exists independently of species.

A wealthy Human and a poor Human may have almost nothing in common.

A wealthy Talassi merchant may employ a poor Human worker.

A Nem artist may be politically powerful but financially unstable.

A Korrun mining family may be respected locally while struggling economically.

Species, nationality and class should never be interchangeable.

# 27. ECONOMIC CONSEQUENCES OF THE LAST EXPEDITION

The Last Expedition changed trade.

Its participants opened:
- new routes;
- ports;
- markets;
- settlements;
- industries.

Some modern families became wealthy because of expedition-era trade.

Others lost everything.

Some modern borders exist because of agreements made during that period.

Some businesses still use names inherited from expedition ships.

The past is economically alive.

---

# 28. ECONOMIC CONSEQUENCES OF THE LAST RETURN

The Last Return caused a new exploration boom.

Effects include:
- expedition companies;
- new shipbuilding;
- mapmaking;
- museums;
- tourism;
- fraudulent businesses;
- government expeditions;
- bounty hunting;
- academic competition;
- religious pilgrimages;
- black markets.

> "I don't care where the famous ship went. I have crops to harvest."

# 29. POVERTY AND WEALTH

Do not turn poverty into aesthetic suffering.

Show:
- people helping one another;
- local institutions;
- ambition;
- humor;
- anger;
- exploitation;
- entrepreneurship;
- family bonds.

Do not turn wealthy people into automatic villains either.

Some wealthy characters can be generous.

Some can be selfish.

Some can be trapped by systems they benefit from.

Class conflict should produce people, not stereotypes.

---

# 30. ECONOMIC STORY ENGINE

Every major location should have at least three economic questions:

1. What does this place produce?
2. What does this place need from elsewhere?
3. Who profits from the difference?

That naturally creates:
- merchants;
- workers;
- smugglers;
- political disputes;
- innovation;
- migration;
- crime;
- alliances.

---

# 31. HOW GOPAL CHANGES ECONOMIES

Gopal should sometimes cause unintended economic consequences.

Examples:

He destroys a criminal warehouse:
- criminals lose inventory;
- legal merchants gain market share;
- workers lose temporary jobs;
- the local government has to investigate.

He saves a port:
- tourism increases;
- businesses profit;
- prices rise;
- locals become annoyed by tourists.

He defeats a corrupt ruler:
- political freedom improves;
- but suddenly nobody knows who will collect taxes.

He helps a small restaurant:
- it becomes famous;
- the owner hates the attention.

These consequences make victories feel real.

---

# 32. THE WORLD SHOULD NOT BE PERFECTLY MODERN

Do not make every modern social/economic institution appear everywhere.

The setting should contain:
- ancient customs;
- new technologies;
- local traditions;
- modern reforms;
- outdated laws;
- experimental institutions.

The world is in transition.

Different people experience different centuries at the same time.

---

# 33. EVERYDAY-LIFE DESIGN TEST

For any major location, answer:

1. What do people eat?
2. Where do they work?
3. How do they travel?
4. How do they communicate?
5. How do children learn?
6. What do people do for fun?
7. What happens when someone gets sick?
8. What happens when someone becomes poor?
9. Who owns property?
10. Who controls trade?
11. What jobs are respected?
12. What jobs are looked down upon?
13. What does the place export?
14. What does it import?
15. What does an ordinary person worry about?
16. What does an ordinary person look forward to?

If we cannot answer these, the location is not finished.

# FINAL PRINCIPLE

> **A world becomes believable when the reader can imagine living there even if Gopal never existed.**

Gopal should be extraordinary.

The world should not need him to be alive.
